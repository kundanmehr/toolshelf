How to Create a Strong Password You Can Actually Remember

Length beats complexity. Learn when to use a random password, when to use a passphrase, and how to stop reusing the same one everywhere.

Most people know they should use strong passwords, but strong ones feel impossible to remember. The good news is that you do not need to remember most of them.

Length matters more than tricks

Swapping an a for an @ does very little. What makes a password hard to guess is length and randomness. For a random password, aim for at least 14 to 16 characters.

Use a passphrase for the few you must remember

For passwords you have to type from memory, such as your password manager or your device, use a passphrase: four or five unrelated words picked at random, like pencil-ocean-rocket-melon. It is long, which makes it strong, and easy to picture, which makes it easy to remember. Pick the words truly at random. A famous quote or a line from a song is much easier for an attacker to guess. Do not use the example above either, since it is now public.

Let a generator handle everything else

For every other account, create a random password with a generator and store it in a password manager. The generator on this site makes passwords inside your browser using its built-in secure random source, and nothing is sent anywhere.

Four habits that matter

  • Never reuse a password. When one site is breached, attackers try the same password on other sites.
  • Avoid personal details. Names, birthdays and phone numbers are among the first things guessed.
  • Turn on two-step verification for your email, banking and social accounts. It protects you even if a password leaks.
  • Change a password when a site reports a breach, not on a fixed schedule for no reason.
